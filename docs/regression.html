2026 Senate: a state-level regression forecast

Predicted Democratic, Republican and Undecided shares in all 35 races

Author

Yi Zhi Zhang

Published

October 30, 2026

NoteReal data

Features come from the Cooperative Election Study (CES, 2014-2024) and the Census ACS 5-year estimates. Targets are CES pre-election Senate intent; accuracy is also checked against certified results from the MIT Election Lab. No 2026 survey exists, so 2026 features carry the 2024 profiles forward.

Summary

  • Coverage: 35 of 35 Senate races have a predicted Democratic, Republican and Undecided share; the three shares sum to 100% in every state (checked).
  • Model: ridge regression on log-ratios of the three shares, using state demographics and party identification (features: ces+prior), trained on 2014, 2014+2016+2018, 2016, 2018, 2020, 2022, 2022+2024, 2024, all, midterms.
  • Predicted winners (model trained on all): 13 Democratic, 22 Republican.
  • Vote counts: Senate seats are decided by statewide popular vote, so a state’s vote share is the right quantity to predict; there is no Electoral College.

Accuracy

Two tests are reported, both with features lagged by two years (the 2024 survey predicts 2026, so the 2022 survey predicts 2024, and so on):

  • Forward-chaining is the honest forecast test: train on earlier cycles only, predict the next one.
  • Leave-one-state-out holds out a state but keeps the other states of the same election in training. It is optimistic, because those states reveal that year’s national swing; it is shown for comparison.

Leakage audit

A forecast may only use what was known before the election. I checked every step of the pipeline for information that would not have been available, and fixed what I found.

Issue What could leak Fix
Same-year features Features and targets came from the same survey year and the same respondents; for 2026 we only have 2024 data. Features are now lagged two years (cycle y uses the survey of y - 2); same-year tables are kept only to measure the leak.
ACS vintage The ACS 5-year estimate for year Y is published after the year-Y election. Lagged with the other features, so only data published before the election are used.
Same-cycle information Leave-one-state-out let other states’ results from the same election (the national swing) inform the held-out state. Kept, labelled optimistic. The headline test is forward-chaining on earlier cycles.
Time travel Leave-one-cycle-out and predict-other-years let later cycles train a model that scored earlier ones. Removed.
Baseline fold The mean-share baseline was computed on all training rows, including the held-out state. Computed inside each fold.
2026 forecast Models trained on same-year features were applied to two-year-old features. The forecast now uses models trained on lagged features.

Checked and clean: certified results are never used to fit a model (only to score it, or as a baseline’s earlier result); ridge penalty and feature scaling are fit inside each training set; every baseline uses only results from years strictly before the target year. One residual: CES case_ids do not repeat across cycles in the cumulative file, so any panel overlap between survey waves cannot be checked.

Simple baselines

Before trusting a regression, compare it with predictions that need no fitting at all. Ideas considered:

  1. Party-ID majority (pid3): the party with more self-identified voters in the state wins.
  2. Party-ID majority with leaners (pid7): the same, but leaners count for their party.
  3. Last Senate result: the state’s most recent certified Senate winner (persistence).
  4. Last presidential result: the state’s most recent presidential winner.
  5. Majority class: every state goes to the party that won most earlier races.
  6. Mean share: every state gets the average two-party share of earlier races.

All six use only information from before the target election, and are scored on exactly the same state-races as the regression (those where every method makes a prediction), against certified results.

Previous results as features

The two baselines that did best, the last presidential and last Senate results, are known before the election, so they can also be features. Two feature sets add them: prior (previous results only, no survey) and ces+prior (survey profile plus previous results). Each state’s last presidential and Senate share come from a year strictly before the target election; where a state had no earlier Senate race, the presidential share is used and a flag marks it.

How the headline model was chosen

The headline forecast is not picked by looking at the final results. It is chosen the standard way: by validation performance, then reported on a holdout cycle that played no part in the choice.

  • Candidates: four feature sets (ces, ces+acs, prior, ces+prior) crossed with three training windows (all earlier cycles, the last two, the last one): 12 models.
  • Validation: each candidate predicts 2018, 2020 and 2022, trained only on earlier cycles (forward-chaining). The winner is the candidate with the lowest error in the Democratic two-party share against certified results (winner accuracy breaks ties).
  • Holdout: 2024, the most recent cycle, is predicted by every candidate for transparency but never used to choose.

The holdout’s own best candidate was prior (all) at 3.3 points; that is expected, because validation picks the best candidate on average, not the best on one cycle. With only three validation cycles and about 33 races each, differences of a point or so between candidates are within noise, so averaging several good models would be a reasonable next step.

Method

  1. Unit of analysis: one row per state per election cycle.
  2. Features (lagged two years): survey-weighted state profile (share male, white, Black, Hispanic, college-educated, mean age, Republican and Democratic party identification), ACS demographics when available, and a midterm-year indicator.
  3. Targets: weighted Democratic, Republican and Undecided/other shares of Senate vote intent, which sum to one. A multi-class regression is fit on additive log-ratios, so predictions stay positive and sum to one.
  4. Penalty: ridge, chosen by leave-one-out cross-validation.
  5. 2026 features: no 2026 survey exists, so the 2024 profiles are carried forward.

Run the whole pipeline with uv run scripts/05-regression.py and rebuild the figures with uv run scripts/06-make_figures.py.

Caveats

  • Targets are survey intent, not votes; “Undecided” includes Not Sure, No One and third-party answers.
  • State profiles come from survey samples; small states have few respondents.
  • Party-identification features come from the same respondents as the targets.
  • No candidate information (incumbency, quality, spending) and no uncertainty intervals yet.
  • Independents who caucus with a party (e.g. Maine, Vermont) are coded by party label.

The full list is in caveats.txt in the repository.
